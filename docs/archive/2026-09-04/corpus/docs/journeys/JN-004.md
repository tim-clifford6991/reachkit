# JN-004 — Changing your account, and leaving

This customer bought weeks ago and has been reading Overview on Mondays since.
They are not exploring. They arrive at Settings with one specific thing they
came to do — the category we inferred is wrong, a competitor they actually lose
deals to is missing, their WordPress token expired, the daily mail is too much,
the card is about to be replaced — and they want it done and to be gone from
this screen. So Settings is short, closed, and honest about consequences: it
holds the three answers the product asked for at setup (JN-002), the two
constraints on pages published under the customer's own name — how those pages
should sound, and what they must never claim — plus account administration, and
nothing that tunes the engine. A customer cannot break their own measurements
here, because the controls that could are not on the screen.

Two of these steps are not on ReachKit's own screens at all. Everything to do
with money — the card, the invoices, the billing details and the cancellation
itself — happens on Stripe's surface, reached from one place in Settings, and
every mail about a payment comes from Stripe rather than from us. What that
leaves ReachKit still owing this customer is the part Stripe holds no fact about:
the day the pages we host for them stop being served, and that their export never
expires. And because this product has no password, the customer who signs out or
opens it on a new machine has one screen to come back to — the only screen in the
product that asks for an address and hands back a link, and the only one a person
with no account can reach without being told first whether an account exists.

The three changes with teeth are the domain, the market and the destination.
Changing the domain changes the website every past number was about, so the
history stays readable as the old site's and the new one starts being measured on
the ordinary weekly cycle rather than the moment of the save. Changing the
market rebuilds the search set and the twelve questions, which is exactly what
a customer with a wrong category wants and exactly what a customer with a right
one must never trigger by accident — so the screen says what it will rebuild
and when, and the question set stays frozen otherwise so week-on-week movement
compares like with like. Both of those changes cost the customer their daily
page for the days in between: nothing new is written until the measurement it
would be chosen from has run, and the customer is told that before they save and
again on each held day, so a quiet gap in the calendar is never something they
have to work out for themselves. A destination whose credentials expired is a state
with a Reconnect button and pages held in the queue, never an error loop and
never lost work. A connection that is live but whose account the customer's own
site will not let publish is a different state again — handing the same password
back would change nothing and no page is waiting — so both what they are told
there and what they are offered have to be different, or the screen sends them
round a loop that cannot end.

The journey also has to end well. A customer who cancels keeps everything
running to the date they paid through, and is told that date. A customer who
unpublishes everything or deletes the account gets their pages handed back
first — every page ReachKit wrote for them, in a form they can use somewhere
else — and unpublishing leaves publishing switched off, so nothing the customer
took down quietly comes back. For the customer on their own WordPress the door
has to close on the right side of it: the posts stay in a site ReachKit no
longer has any way to show them, so the last thing ReachKit says has to leave
them able to find their own work in one place they can open, or say plainly that
their site offers no such place — never leave them opening posts one at a time
to see which were ours. That matters because the product's claim is that the content is theirs, and a claim
that only holds while you are paying is not a claim. That claim only means
something if the door still opens afterwards, so the path does not end at the
moment of leaving: a customer whose plan lapsed months ago can still ask for a
sign-in link, sign in, and either take their pages or start again on the same
domain. The journey succeeds either way: the customer who stays got their one
change and left the screen in under a minute, and the customer who left took
their work with them.
