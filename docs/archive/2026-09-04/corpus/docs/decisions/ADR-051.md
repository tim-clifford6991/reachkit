# ADR-051 — LANDMINE — deletion is a tombstone plus a 30-day purge, and unreachability is enforced by the row policy, not by callers

- Date: 2026-08-31

> **Read this before adding a cascade delete, before "finishing" the delete path,
> and before adding `deleted_at is null` to a query.** All three read as tidying up.
> The first destroys data the product has promised to still hold; the third is how
> the guarantee silently stops holding.

## Context
REQ-079 criterion 7 asks for two different things at two different times:

> when the customer or anyone else reaches for its data through any product
> surface — sign-in, export, a published page's record — then **none of it is
> reachable from the moment of deletion**; and when **30 days have passed**, then
> the account and the sign-in address it was reached at, the site, its pages,
> drafts, measurements, destination credentials and every copy ReachKit holds of
> the customer's own page content … are **no longer present** in ReachKit's stored
> data at all.

Unreachable now; absent in thirty days. Those are two mechanisms, not one, and the
gap between them holds rows that exist and must not be readable.

**Why the gap looks like an unfinished delete.** "Delete account" that leaves the
account row in place, with the email still in the unique index, reads as a
half-written feature. The obvious completion is `ON DELETE CASCADE` from `users`
down through `sites`, `drafts`, `publications`, `destinations` — one migration, and
the code gets shorter.

**Why the gap is load-bearing.** Three promises live inside it, each made by a
different blueprint:

1. **REQ-079 criterion 6** — where a destination could not be reached, one `account`
   mail names the pages left live there and what the customer must do to remove
   them. That mail is sent to the address being deleted, about pages recorded in
   rows that a cascade would already have taken. A cascade turns criterion 6 into an
   email nobody can compose.
2. **REQ-076 criterion 10 and REQ-079 criteria 4 and 6** — a hosted address that
   served a page must answer **410 Gone**, "rather than a page, a redirect or a
   not-found". BP-004 can only tell 410 from 404 if something still records that the
   address once existed. With the rows gone, every deleted customer's pages answer
   404, which is a different statement to a crawler and a different statement to a
   reader.
3. **The 30 days is the erasure window the product states**, and states as an
   outer bound the customer can hold ReachKit to. Erasing earlier is not more
   compliant; it removes the record that proves what was erased and when, and it
   removes the operational ability to answer "what happened to my pages" in the week
   after somebody's colleague clicked the button.

**Why the third instinct — `deleted_at is null` in each query — is the worse fix.**
Deletion has to hold across sign-in (BP-061), export (BP-062), every app surface
(BP-001), the hosted edge (BP-004) and every scheduled loop (BP-003). A predicate
repeated in five subsystems written by five agents is a predicate one of them will
omit, and the omission reads as a working feature until a deleted customer's page is
served to a stranger.

No single blueprint owns this. BP-063 performs the deletion, BP-002 owns the row
policies, BP-004 owns the edge that must answer 410, BP-062 owns the export that
must refuse, BP-060 owns the serving state. That is what earns a file (rule 2.2),
and being a landmine earns it regardless.

## Decision
**Deletion is a stamp; absence is a job; unreachability is a database policy.**

1. `deleteAccount` sets `users.deleted_at = now()` and `users.purge_due_at =
   now() + ERASURE_DAYS` (BP-063). It issues no `DELETE` of any kind.
2. **No foreign key in this schema carries `ON DELETE CASCADE` from `users` or
   `sites`.** Cascades are how the tombstone becomes a hard delete by accident, one
   migration at a time.
3. **Unreachability is enforced in BP-002's RLS policies**, not in application code:
   every request-scoped policy that already restricts a row to its owning user gains
   the condition that the owning user's `deleted_at` is null. The `dbAdmin()` client
   bypasses RLS by design and is the only way BP-063's own mail composition and
   BP-003's purge reach a tombstoned account — which is precisely the narrow, named
   exception the design needs.
4. **BP-004 asks BP-060's `hostedServingState`**, which answers `serve: false,
   because: 'account_deleted'` while the tombstone stands, and the edge answers 410
   (BP-060 decision 1). After the purge the site row is gone and the address answers
   404; by then the 410 has stood for thirty days, which is longer than any crawler
   needs to drop it.
5. **The email stays in `users` and stays unique** during the window, so
   `requestMagicLink` finds no account (the policy hides it) while
   `beginEmailChange` still refuses to move another customer onto it (BP-061's
   in-use check reads through `dbAdmin()`). Both behaviours are required and they
   are only compatible because the row is present but hidden.
6. **The purge is the only deleter.** `purgeAccount` removes, in dependency order
   and idempotently, the account and its address, the site, pages, drafts,
   measurements, destination credentials and every copy of the customer's own page
   content — including `drafts.grounded_fact`, whose retention under REQ-050
   criterion 1 ends here.
7. Tests that discriminate: a tombstoned account is invisible to sign-in, to export
   and to every app read **through `db()`**, and is still visible through
   `dbAdmin()`; a hosted address answers 410 while tombstoned and 404 after the
   purge; and a test asserts that no `ON DELETE CASCADE` exists on a `users` or
   `sites` foreign key in the applied schema.

## Alternatives considered
1. **Hard delete with cascades at the moment of deletion** — rejected: it satisfies
   the "no longer present" half early and fails criterion 6's mail, the 410, and the
   ability to say anything at all about what was removed.
2. **`deleted_at is null` added to each caller's queries** — rejected: five
   subsystems, five chances to omit it, and the omission is invisible until it is a
   breach.
3. **Move the row to an archive schema at deletion** — rejected: it is a cascade with
   extra steps, it breaks the unique index that keeps a deleted address from being
   handed to another customer, and it needs its own purge anyway.
4. **A shorter window (7 days) so the exposure is smaller** — rejected: 30 days is on
   the record as an owner ruling, and the window is a pin (`ERASURE_DAYS`), not a
   structure. Changing it is one number; changing the mechanism is this file.

## Consequences
- For thirty days, a deleted customer's rows exist and are unreadable through the
  request-scoped client. That is a state a reader of the schema will find surprising;
  this file is the answer to "why is this still here".
- The RLS condition is BP-002's to write, and it is the single point of failure for
  the whole guarantee. It gets its own test that fails if the condition is removed
  from any policy, not one that merely passes on the happy path.
- Reversing this after launch means either accepting an unrecoverable early erasure
  (a cascade) or auditing five subsystems for a predicate. Both cost more than this
  file.
- Rows within the window are still ReachKit's responsibility for backup, encryption
  and access. Nothing here weakens that; the point is that they are unreachable
  through the product, not that they are unimportant.
