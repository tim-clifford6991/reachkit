# ADR-080 — LANDMINE — the publication row and the destination-side marker are the at-most-once guarantee, and neither may be tidied away

- Date: 2026-08-31

> **Read this before deleting a `publications` row, before adding
> `on delete cascade` to one, before "cleaning up" rows for pages that were
> never published or that the customer took down, and before changing what
> BP-048 writes onto a post it creates.** Every one of those reads as
> housekeeping. Each of them silently re-arms the duplicate post that REQ-056
> criterion 5 exists to make impossible — and none of them makes any test fail,
> because the thing they remove is a guard, not a behaviour.

## Context
REQ-056 criterion 5 is the promise: "Given a page and a destination for which a
publish has already been attempted, when a publish is attempted again —
including a retry of an attempt whose outcome was never confirmed, and an
attempt made after the page was unpublished — then at most one post for that
page exists at that destination, and a page in `unpublished` gains no post."
`BUILD.md` §9 states the mechanism in one line: "Publishing idempotent by
`(draft_id, destination)` — a retry can never create a second post."

BP-015 decision 2 accordingly fixes that the publication row is inserted first,
inside the transaction that claims the attempt, and that a conflicting insert
means the page is already there. BP-045 refines which row "already there" means
(a row whose `delivery_state` is `delivered`). That is half of the guarantee.

The other half is at a destination we do not own. The unique index protects a
table in our database; it cannot see inside a customer's WordPress. The case
criterion 5 names explicitly — "a retry of an attempt whose outcome was never
confirmed" — is precisely the case where we called the destination, it created
the post, and we lost the answer. Only something we left *at the destination*
can tell a later attempt that the post already exists, which is why BP-048
writes a marker on the post it creates and searches for it before creating one.

So there are exactly two guards, and both of them are inert data:

| Guard | Where | What it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| The `publications` row under `unique (draft_id, destination_id)` | our database | A second delivery for a page/destination pair, including after the page was unpublished |
| The marker on the post BP-048 created | the customer's WordPress | A second post after a delivery whose outcome we never recorded |

**Why they read as wrong.** Both look like residue.

- A publication row exists for a page that is *not* published — it was claimed
  and the attempt failed. To a reader, a `publications` row for an unpublished
  page is an inconsistency, and the obvious fixes are "delete the row on
  failure", "insert the row only after the destination confirms", or
  "`on conflict do nothing`".
- A publication row exists for a page the customer *took down*. REQ-056 criterion
  3 makes `unpublished` terminal and the page is gone from the customer's site;
  keeping a row that says it was published there reads like stale data, and
  deleting it on unpublish reads like correctness.
- `on delete cascade` from `destinations` or `drafts` to `publications` is the
  default a schema reviewer asks for, and REQ-074 criterion 4's "stored
  credentials are destroyed" invites deleting the destination row with them.
- The marker on the WordPress post looks like our metadata polluting somebody
  else's site, and REQ-060's non-goals ("editing existing WordPress posts") and
  REQ-056 criterion 16 ("ReachKit never made it live", "touches nothing") read
  as an argument for writing as little as possible there.

**Why they are load-bearing.** Each of those changes deletes the only evidence a
future attempt has:

- Inserting the row *after* the destination confirms loses exactly the race
  criterion 5 names — the window between the destination creating the post and
  us learning it did — which BP-015 decision 2 already rejected once.
- Deleting the row on failure means the next retry is a first attempt, and if
  the failed attempt had in fact delivered, the retry creates the second post.
- Deleting the row on unpublish means "a page in `unpublished` gains no post"
  rests on nothing: the state is terminal, so no *transition* can start an
  attempt, but any operational replay, backfill or manual re-drive of a job then
  has nothing to stop it.
- Cascading the delete from `destinations` means a disconnect-and-reconnect
  cycle — which REQ-074 criteria 3 and 4 make an ordinary customer action, and
  which BP-035 makes the way a customer changes destination — starts every page
  over as a first attempt against a site that already holds the posts.
- Removing the marker leaves the WordPress adapter with no way to distinguish
  "never delivered" from "delivered, answer lost", and the only safe behaviour
  left would be to never retry at all — which contradicts REQ-056 criterion 4.

**And nothing fails when they are removed.** That is what makes this a landmine
rather than a preference. Every acceptance test still passes: the happy path
publishes once, the retry path retries, the unpublish path unpublishes. The
guard only ever matters in the interleaving nobody reproduces by hand, and its
absence surfaces as a customer's blog with the same article on it twice — which
is the failure REQ-056's user story names first ("never have to clean up after
the system").

## Decision
1. **A `publications` row is written before the destination is called, never
   after**, inside the claiming transaction, with `delivery_state='claimed'`
   (BP-045). The destination call happens after that transaction commits.
2. **A publication row is never deleted to reflect an outcome.** Not on failure,
   not on `needs_attention`, not on unpublish, not on disconnect, not by any
   cleanup, backfill or maintenance task. Outcomes are recorded by updating
   `delivery_state`, `failure_reason` and `unpublished_at`.
3. **No foreign key into `publications` carries `on delete cascade`** from
   `drafts`, `destinations` or `sites` (BP-002, BP-058). A destination that is
   disconnected keeps its row with `deleted_at` set and its ciphertext nulled
   (BP-058 decision 3); its publications stay.
4. **The conflict clause refuses only against a delivered row.** The claim is
   `on conflict (draft_id, destination_id) do update … where
   publications.delivery_state <> 'delivered'`. `do nothing` would refuse the
   legitimate retry; an unconditional `do update` would allow the duplicate.
5. **BP-048 writes a marker naming the draft on every post it creates, and
   searches for that marker before creating one.** The marker is not decoration
   and is not removable; it is the only at-most-once mechanism available at a
   destination whose database we cannot index. Changing the marker's shape is a
   migration of somebody else's site and requires a fallback that finds the old
   shape first.
6. **The one deletion that is correct is the whole-account purge.** REQ-079
   criterion 7 requires that 30 days after deletion "the account and the sign-in
   address it was reached at, the site, its pages, drafts, measurements,
   destination credentials and every copy ReachKit holds" are no longer present.
   That purge removes the destination and the site along with the publications,
   so no future attempt against that pair can exist; it is a deletion of the
   whole subject, not a tidy-up of a guard. BP-017's purge is therefore
   permitted, and it is the **only** path that may delete a `publications` row.
7. **A test asserts each guard by its absence, not by the happy path.** The
   discriminating tests are: a retry after a delivery whose outcome was never
   recorded creates no second post; an attempt against an unpublished page
   creates no post; a disconnect-and-reconnect creates no second post. Deleting
   the guard must make one of them fail — that is the mutation check §8 requires,
   and it is the reason those three tests exist at all.

## Alternatives considered
1. **Application-level "have we published this?" before calling the
   destination** — rejected in BP-015 decision 2 and rejected again here: it
   loses the race in the read-then-write gap, and it is the design every one of
   the tidy-ups above degrades into.
2. **Insert the publication row only on a confirmed delivery** — rejected: it is
   the same race, and it is the specific shape a reviewer will propose because
   it makes the table read consistently. Consistency of the table is not the
   property criterion 5 asks for.
3. **A separate `publish_attempts` table, with `publications` holding only real
   publications** — rejected: it splits one fact across two rows, the unique
   constraint would have to move to the attempts table (making `publications`
   decorative), and `BUILD.md` §10's tenth-table test is not met. It also does
   not help the WordPress half at all.
4. **No destination-side marker; refuse to retry a WordPress delivery whose
   outcome is unknown** — rejected: it converts every lost answer into a page
   that needs the customer, and REQ-056 criterion 4 promises automatic recovery
   from reasons a repeated attempt could clear.
5. **Record this inside BP-045's `## Decisions`** — rejected under rule 2.2's
   file test. The invariant has one half in BP-045's claim, one half in BP-048's
   adapter, one clause in BP-058's disconnect, one absent `on delete cascade` in
   BP-002's schema and one permitted exception in BP-017's purge. No single
   blueprint owns it, and the reader most likely to break it is the one editing
   a migration or an erasure path, who has no reason to open BP-045. It also
   **amends** BP-015 decision 2 by fixing which row "already there" means and by
   adding the retention rule that decision leaves unstated — the second of rule
   2.2's two file tests.

## Consequences
- `publications` accumulates rows for attempts that never delivered. That is
  correct and intended; `delivery_state` is what distinguishes them, and every
  read path that means "published" filters on it rather than on the row's
  existence.
- Storage grows with attempts rather than with publications. At one publish per
  day and at most four attempts per publication, this is bounded by design and
  needs no compaction — and a compaction job would be this ADR's forbidden
  cleanup wearing a different hat.
- A customer who disconnects and reconnects a destination keeps their publishing
  history and their held queue, which is what REQ-074 criterion 3's "how many
  pages are waiting" counts.
- BP-048 writes one marker into the customer's site on each post it creates. It
  is on our own post, at creation, and never on a post we did not create, so
  REQ-060's non-goals and REQ-056 criterion 16 are untouched.
- **Cost of reversing:** near zero in code — a `delete`, a cascade, a changed
  conflict clause — and that cheapness is the danger. The true cost is a
  duplicate article on a paying customer's own blog, appearing only in the
  interleaving nobody reproduces, in the one place `BUILD.md` §9 says the
  product "touches the customer's own site". Anyone who finds a publication row
  for an unpublished page and thinks "this is stale data" should read this file
  first, which is the entire reason it is a file.
