# ADR-041 — LANDMINE — a nurture sequence that missed its window is dropped forever, a re-delivery starts nothing, and an opted-out address still gets the page it asked for

- Date: 2026-08-31

> **Read this before releasing a waiting sequence, before "fixing" a lead who
> received no follow-ups, and before adding a suppression check to the giveaway
> send.** All three look like bugs. All three are REQ-010, and each of them is
> the only thing standing between this product and the mailing list its user
> story says a founder must not regret.

## Context
REQ-010 trades a finished page for an email address and then bounds what may
follow. Three of its clauses produce states that read, from inside the code or
from a support inbox, exactly like defects:

1. **Criterion 12** — "A sequence that has not begun within 7 days of its own
   page's delivery is dropped and never sent." A lead who scanned four domains in
   a week ends with one sequence delivered and three rows in a terminal
   `dropped` state having sent nothing. In the database this is indistinguishable
   from a scheduler that fell over, and the fix an engineer reaches for — release
   the waiting rows now, they only missed their slot — is one line.
2. **Criterion 13** — a domain's page delivered again to the same address starts
   no second sequence and the touches stay capped at three. The second `leads`
   row has null sequence state forever. It reads as a lost enqueue.
3. **Criterion 3 against criterion 11** — an address on `email_suppressions`
   that submits the capture control for a *different* domain still receives that
   domain's page. It reads as the one thing no mail system may ever do: send to
   someone who opted out.

Each fix is honest-looking, small, and destroys a promise the requirement makes
in its own user story.

Releasing dropped sequences breaks the only bound REQ-010 places on elapsed
time: the criterion's own arithmetic is that a start no later than 7 days plus a
last touch at 168 hours is "no follow-up about a domain ever reaches an address
more than 14 days after that domain's page was delivered to it". A released
sequence mails a stranger about a scan they have forgotten, which is the
mailing-list-I-regret outcome the story names.

Un-capping re-delivery breaks the one hard number a founder is told before they
type: criterion 14's line promises "the page and up to three further emails about
that domain". A second sequence makes it six, and the founder learns the real
size of the trade from the mails, which criterion 14's last clause forbids in so
many words.

Adding a blanket suppression check to the giveaway send breaks criterion 3 —
"a mail the founder cannot stop: it is the thing they asked for, sent once, in
answer to their own submission" — and with it criterion 7, whose written
explanation of why a page is not coming is unstoppable on the same grounds. An
opt-out in REQ-010 is scoped to *follow-up*: criterion 11 stops "all future
follow-up email", enumerated as the running sequence's remaining touches, other
domains' sequences, and sequences a future delivery would start. It does not
reach a page the person has just asked for by typing their address into a
control that says what it does.

## Decision
**All three states are correct and are enforced structurally rather than by a
check that a later reader could take for a mistake.**

1. A waiting sequence past `page_delivered_at + SEQUENCE_START_DEADLINE_DAYS` is
   swept to `dropped` by `advanceSequences()` **before** any release step, on
   every invocation, so the terminal state is reached even on a run that
   releases nothing. There is no code path from `dropped` to `running`, and
   `dropped_at` records when the bound was applied. The 14-day guarantee is
   arithmetic over two pins in BP-005 rather than a separate check that could
   drift from them.
2. Criterion 13 is a **unique index**, not a branch:
   `unique (lower(email), domain) where sequence_state is not null`, over a
   column that is set once and whose every later value is terminal. A second
   sequence for a domain an address already has one for is unrepresentable, so
   the "missing enqueue" cannot be restored by calling the scheduler again.
3. The giveaway kinds are registered `stoppable: false`
   (`first-page`, `first-page-unavailable`), which is what tells BP-053's send
   seam not to consult `email_suppressions` for them. `scheduleSequence()`
   consults it and refuses. The suppression check therefore exists, in one
   place, and reaches exactly the follow-up — and every one of these mails,
   including the unstoppable page, carries the opt-out link (criterion 11's
   "any of these emails"), so a founder who wants no follow-up has the control
   in their hand in the same mail.

## Alternatives considered
1. **Release a waiting sequence late, with a shortened touch schedule** —
   rejected. It preserves the row and breaks the promise: criterion 12's bound is
   on elapsed time since delivery, not on the number of touches, and a compressed
   schedule still lands mail about a two-week-old scan.
2. **Let a re-delivery restart the sequence and rely on `touch_count` to cap the
   total at three** — rejected. It is defensible against criterion 9's number and
   fails criterion 13's clause about a sequence "already finished", and it makes
   the cap a running total maintained by application code where the requirement
   states a uniqueness property.
3. **Suppress the giveaway page for an opted-out address and show the founder a
   line on the report instead** — rejected. It is criterion 7's failure path used
   to paper over a success, it means the product refuses to hand over the one
   thing it just asked for an address in exchange for, and it silently makes the
   opt-out irreversible for a person who has since decided they want a page.
4. **Record all three as inline decisions in BP-029** — rejected under this
   factory's landmine rule. Each of these reads as wrong to a reader who has the
   code and not the requirement, and the reversal in every case looks like a
   cleanup and quietly removes a bound. The reader who needs this is looking at
   a `dropped` row or a support ticket, not at a blueprint's fifth decision.

## Consequences
- Three states in `leads` that will be asked about: `dropped` rows that sent
  nothing, null-sequence rows for repeat domains, and delivered mail to
  suppressed addresses. Each has a log line naming the rule and this ADR.
- Two of the three are enforced by things that cannot be forgotten — an index
  and a register row — and the third by ordering inside one function.
- A support answer exists for each: the founder was told the size of the trade
  before they typed (criterion 14), and every mail carries the opt-out.
- Cost of reversing: the code cost is one line in each case, which is precisely
  the danger. The behavioural cost is REQ-010's user story — a useful trade
  becoming a mailing list — and it is invisible in tests written from the code
  rather than from the criteria. Any change to these three must cite REQ-010
  criteria 3, 11, 12 and 13 and supersede this decision.
